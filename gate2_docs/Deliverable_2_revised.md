**Problem, Solution, Strategy, Financials and Execution Plan**
Going into Gate 2 we have narrowed our problem statement and settled on a product. For students and young professionals in Boston who want a good place to eat without checking three apps first, our app is a free, all-in-one restaurant discovery guide that puts the reviews, social posts, hours and booking for each place on one page. Instead of checking Google Maps, Instagram or TikTok, and a friend separately, people can research, save and share in one app, with a score that counts only verified visits. The app combines maps, restaurant profiles, social content, and personal lists. 
Since September we have interviewed around 35 students and young professionals. Most check three sources before choosing a restaurant: Google Maps for options, Instagram or TikTok to see the food and atmosphere, and a friend to confirm it is good. Many said that, even after all three, the restaurant they chose was sometimes disappointing. Most were satisfied enough with what they use today, but said they would try something better. That points to interest in a more consolidated experience, although it does not yet establish product market fit or willingness to pay. 
**Target Customer and Market Positioning**
Our initial beachhead is students and young professionals in Boston, beginning at Northeastern. This audience frequently discovers restaurants through social channels, recommendations, and review platforms and is reachable through campus organizations, group chats and through our peers. Our Gate 2 research estimates 162,458 college students and 3,350 licensed food establishments in Boston. Our market model defines a broader US TAM of 77.8 million adults between the ages of 18-34, a SAM of 19 million US college students and a Year 5 SOM of 540,000 active users, or 2.8% of the SAM. 
**Competitive Differentiation **
The prototype shows a web and mobile experience featuring neighborhood maps, nearby restaurants, cuisine and dietary filters, price and distance filters, opening hours, and personalized lists. Each Restaurant profile is designed to bring together relevant Google Maps ratings, Instagram posts, TikTok videos, and Beli reviews so users can compare multiple perspectives instead of relying on a single score. The proposed integrations are a product vision; access to third-party content and live data would depend on available permissions, licensing and technical feasibility. Users would also maintain a personal dining history and write reviews that are automatically saved to their accounts. Those reviews would remain private or anonymous by default; users could choose to publish them for friends to see. The proposed in-app public rating system would count a score only after a receipt verifies the visit, while external platform reviews would remain clearly attributed to their original sources. Receipt verification helps establish that a visit occurred, but does not guarantee an unbiased opinion. Restaurants cannot pay for higher rankings. Google Maps and Yelp provide broad listings and reviews; Instagram and TikTok offer visual discovery; Beli focuses on personal restaurant rankings. Our intended advantage is combining those fragmented research activities with personal organization and an additional verified-review layer. Our main challenge is convincing people that one app is more useful than the familiar services they already use.
**Marketing **
 Our core marketing messages are “One app instead of three” and “See the food, reviews, and recommendations in one place.” Messaging will focus on less searching and better choices. To reach our first 10 users, we will return to interview participants and friends at Northeastern for prototype feedback. The first 100 users will come through campus clubs, group chats, and invitations from early testers. The first 1,000 will be pursued through Boston campus ambassadors and restaurant focused Instagram and TikTok content. Our model relies heavily on peer referral growth. The consumer funnel is awareness through a friend or post, app visit or download, first restaurant search, profile exploration, save or personal review, repeat use, and optional sharing or referral. For restaurant partners, the founders handle sales directly. The funnel moves from outreach, beginning with five owner calls, to a free 30-day pilot that reports the verified diners we sent, then a per-diner agreement and renewal. We will measure activation, repeat use, retention, verified reviews, referral conversion, cost per acquired user, and paying restaurant partners. Downloads alone will not be treated as a measure of success. 
**Business model **
Users never have to pay. The core discovery and personal-review experience is free, including dietary filters, because a search for a halal or nut-free place is what sends restaurants the diners they pay for. Revenue comes from two sides. On the business side, restaurants pay approximately $0.90 for each verified diner the app sends them, our main revenue line; booking partners pay a referral fee for each table; and from Year 3, chains pay for area-level dining insights. On the user side, a small share of users may choose an optional $5-per-month membership, whose benefits are not yet final. The app accepts no sponsorships, promotions or paid placement, so nothing a business pays for affects results or scores.
The team projects 12,000 active users at the end of Year 1 and about 540,000 by Year 5. Revenue is projected at $12,000 in Year 2, $50,400 in Year 3, $140,200 in Year 4 and $355,500 in Year 5. The model forecasts $72,100 in net income in Year 5, our first profitable year, while cumulative results remain a loss of approximately $119,600 at that point. Funding totals $240,000: about $74,000 from grants and pitch competitions for Years 1 and 2, with the founders covering any shortfall, and about $166,000 from one angel round at the end of Year 2, once restaurants are paying. This total equals the lowest cumulative loss, about $191,800 in Year 4, plus a 25% cushion. The plan assumes no founder salaries and a $6,000 annual marketing budget.
These estimates are untested, especially the low acquisition costs and fast organic growth they imply. Next, we will test whether people prefer the consolidated restaurant page, return to their saved lists, write private reviews, share with friends and accept receipt verification, and whether restaurants will pay for verified visits. The plan depends on users returning and restaurants paying.
**Why a restaurant would pay**
Restaurants already spend money to bring people in, on ads, boosted posts and deal apps, and most of it is paid up front with no way to tell who came because of it. We would charge differently: a restaurant pays about $0.90 only for each diner we sent whose receipt shows they came, below the $1.00 to $1.50 OpenTable charges for each seated diner it sends (OpenTable pricing via Eat App, 2026). New restaurants do not pay to be listed; the app surfaces a new restaurant at no cost once it attracts attention online, which matters in a city that added 279 food licenses last year (City of Boston open data). Verified reviews also benefit restaurants. The FTC banned fake reviews in 2024, and a score that needs a receipt is much harder for a competitor to fake. We have not yet interviewed restaurant owners, so this reflects our understanding of their problem, and it is the next assumption we will test.
**Where the money goes in Years 1 and 2**
We plan to spend about $10,500 a quarter in fixed costs: $7,500 for a contract developer to build and run the app, $1,500 for campus marketing, and $1,500 for legal, accounting and insurance. Founders take no salary. Spending stays flat until Year 3, when co-op students join as the user base grows. The five-year plan is in our separate financial plan, and every input and its source is in the Excel model.
**Why we think this will work**
Our interviews indicate that students already research restaurants across three sources, so the behavior already exists. Beli shows that a food app for people our age can grow mostly through friends: it went from about 200,000 to 1.1 million monthly users in under two years (Sensor Tower, Boston Globe, Nov 2025), with about 80% of growth from referrals (Wall Street Journal, May 2025). We start on our own campus, where we can reach the first users ourselves, and we keep costs low enough that about $240,000 carries us to our first profitable year, Year 5. The app also becomes harder to copy as it grows: every verified review makes the scores more useful, which brings in more users, and each person's lists and dining history stay with the app.
